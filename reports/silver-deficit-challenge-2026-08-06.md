# Silver's Deficit: Persisting Without Demand Growth

**Thesis.** The available aggregate data no longer support describing the 2025–2026 silver deficits as primarily the result of rising industrial demand. Reported industrial demand contracted 3% in 2025 and is forecast to contract a further 3% in 2026 (World Silver Survey 2026, April 2026), while the market recorded a fifth consecutive deficit of 40.3 million ounces in 2025 and is forecast a sixth of 46.3 million ounces in 2026. Whether the deficit has fully changed character is not yet established; what the evidence supports is narrower: aggregate industrial-demand growth is not the incremental driver of the 2025 actual and 2026 forecast deficits, and the replacement explanation is a working hypothesis to be tested — not a concluded finding. This memo challenges the framing of the published Silver note and updates it with the Survey's newer data. The CRO's strongest opposing view is published as a companion.

## The arithmetic problem in the original note

The published note's valuation anchor is internally inconsistent. It cites gold at approximately $4,206.60 per ounce and a gold/silver ratio near 88:1 while also describing silver as trading in the "low $20s." The arithmetic is unambiguous: $4,206.60 divided by 88 implies silver at approximately $47.80 per ounce; silver at $24 would imply a ratio near 175:1, and silver at $22 near 191:1. The ratio claim and the low-$20s price claim cannot describe the same synchronized observation — at least one of the gold price, silver price, ratio convention, or timestamp is not synchronized.

The honest conclusion is limited: relative cheapness remains unproven on the note's own inputs. Correcting the mismatch could cut either way — a 175:1 ratio would make silver cheaper than the note claimed; an 88:1 ratio with silver near $47.80 would make it less cheap. Until the current silver fixing is re-pulled, the "cheap relative to gold" framing should be treated as unresolved rather than accepted. The gold reference itself — LBMA PM gold of $4,206.60 on 5 August 2026, up 24.6% year over year — also raises the possibility that the ratio is being driven as much by gold's repricing as by silver-specific dislocation. (Percentage changes are source-attributed — Survey/LBMA/FRED — and were not independently re-derived from underlying levels in this review.)

## The demand side: the deficit persists without demand growth

The World Silver Survey 2026 reports the 40.3 million-ounce deficit in 2025 — the fifth consecutive annual deficit — even though total demand fell 2% and industrial demand fell 3%. It forecasts industrial demand falling another 3% in 2026 while the market records a sixth deficit of 46.3 million ounces. Those figures directly challenge the proposition that the current deficit is being generated by growth in the industrial demand base. If industrial consumption is contracting at the aggregate level, candidate explanations to be tested include some combination of weak supply response, investment absorption through bars and coins, reduced recycling, and inventory movements — a working hypothesis, not yet quantified. (The audit required this phrasing: the categorical "must lie" of the first draft overstated what the aggregate data can prove.)

A distinction the original note does not draw matters here: the **drivers of the accounting deficit** — mine supply, recycling, industrial demand, and investment demand — are not the same as the **mechanisms through which that deficit clears** — inventory release, metal relocation, financing, and liquidity. Investment demand can enter the balance directly as a deficit driver; inventory location and liquidity mostly determine availability and market stress, not the annual accounting balance itself. Conflating the two is how "deficit" gets mistaken for "scarcity."

There remains a fair counter-reading, and it should be stated fairly. Solar fabrication could be growing in installation terms while thrifting reduces silver use per unit, or solar demand could remain strong while weakness in other industrial categories pulls the aggregate lower. Under that interpretation, solar still supports the long-run demand base even if it is not producing aggregate industrial growth in 2025–26. But the note's specific claims — record solar demand, approximately 700,000 ounces per gigawatt, 20% year-over-year growth — cannot be tested against the Survey's aggregate without the solar-specific 2025 actual split. The missing split prevents attribution; it is not evidence that solar failed to grow. Solar may therefore remain an important structural theme, but the available evidence does not establish it as the proximate explanation for the current deficit.

## The supply and cost side: inelastic, and cheaper than the note said

The Survey forecasts 2026 mine production of 844.1 million ounces, down 0.3% — a market whose output responds slowly (the direct 2025 actual production level was not available in this review's sources). Because much silver is produced as a by-product of lead, zinc, copper, or gold mining, silver prices alone do not trigger timely capacity additions or closures. That structural inelasticity can allow deficits to persist even as industrial demand falls. (The by-product share assertion is widely documented but lacks a discrete source locator in this review — flagged for retrieval.)

On costs: the note's approximately $14-per-ounce all-in sustaining cost estimate sits above the Survey's reported 2025 primary-mine AISC of $12.21 per ounce, down 1% — a spread of roughly $1.79. The reported primary-mine AISC weighs against broad cost-driven destruction among primary producers; it does not exclude cost-related supply effects elsewhere — by-product output economics, recycling, marginal projects, or individual high-cost mines. Falling primary-mine costs also weaken any argument that the deficit is being created by cost-driven primary supply destruction: at $12.21, the primary cost base sits well below either price reference in the note.

## The liquidity side: the squeeze that resolved

The note treats a Shanghai premium of roughly $2 per ounce over London and purported COMEX paper-market discounting as signs that physical tightness is leading financial pricing. Those are target-note claims (pipeline artifact 2026-08-05) — the premium's exact observation date, benchmark pair, and the COMEX discount's contract and calculation method are unverified in this review. The World Silver Survey 2026 states that the October 2025 liquidity squeeze subsequently eased as metal returned to London. A resolved squeeze demonstrates that acute tightness can reflect metal location, financing conditions, and deliverability rather than irreversible depletion — which weakens treating any single premium as proof of a persistent structural shortage. It does not refute the possibility that premiums contain forward information. Regional premiums should therefore be treated as corroborating indicators whose persistence — and relationship to inventories and lease rates — must be tested, not as standalone proof.

## What to watch

The watch framework shifts from isolated premium observations to a reconciled set of stock-and-flow measures — the evidence required to test the hypothesis, not proof that it is already confirmed:

- The inventory-and-liquidity interpretation would be supported if London available stocks and COMEX deliverable inventories decline persistently, lease rates remain elevated, and regional physical premiums recur or persist despite normalized logistics; strengthened further if ETF, coin, and bar absorption accounts for the deficit while industrial demand remains negative.
- It would be weakened if vault availability rebuilds, lease rates and premiums normalize, and investment flows fail to absorb metal — in which case the reported deficit would have limited implications for near-term scarcity.
- A renewed rise in verified solar and broader industrial offtake sufficient to reverse the aggregate contraction would restore demand growth as the principal explanation.
- A synchronized silver fixing (LBMA) must be re-pulled to resolve the valuation anchor before any relative-value claim is made.

## The dissenting view

The Chief Risk Officer's strongest opposing essay is published as a companion: *The Case for the Silver Thesis's Survival*. Its central argument: the ratio/price mismatch is most plausibly a timestamp failure — and if the current ratio is actually ~175:1 (silver near $24, gold $4,206), silver is *cheaper* than the note claimed; a deficit persisting under falling demand and falling costs is precisely what structural supply inelasticity predicts, so the reclassification strengthens rather than breaks the original supply argument; and a resolved squeeze does not negate tightness — recurring premiums and inventory drawdowns would show the liquid buffer being progressively exhausted. The CRO's "silver is unusually cheap" conclusion relies on the ~65:1 historical median, whose sample period and methodology remain unverified in this review — treat it as a preserved opposing view, not the audited base-case conclusion.
