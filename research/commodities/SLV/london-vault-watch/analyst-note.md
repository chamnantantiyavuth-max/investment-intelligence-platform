**London Silver Vault Rebuild Weakens the Inventory-Depletion Thesis, but Does Not Yet Establish Liquidity Normalization**

The June 2026 London vault reading materially weakens the published silver challenge memo's inventory-and-liquidity hypothesis on its first explicit watch item. LBMA-reported silver holdings reached a series high of 902.843 million troy ounces, up 137.954 Moz, or 18.04%, from June 2025. That is not the persistent decline in London stocks contemplated by the memo's confirmation case; it is a substantial visible-stock rebuild and therefore activates one element of the memo's own weakening condition. The appropriate conclusion is narrower than declaring the physical-deficit thesis invalid. Reported vault holdings are not equivalent to immediately available, unencumbered metal, and the broader break condition also requires normalization in lease rates and physical premiums, alongside insufficient investment absorption. Nevertheless, the burden of proof has shifted: an interpretation centered on depletion of London metal can no longer rest on the aggregate vault series alone.

The scale and timing of the rebuild are economically significant. London silver holdings increased from 764.889 Moz in June 2025 to 902.843 Moz in June 2026, a gain of approximately 138 Moz. That increase is more than three times the reported 40.3 Moz silver-market deficit for 2025 and exceeds the combined 2025 and forecast 2026 deficits of 86.6 Moz. June's reported stock was approximately 2.5 times those two deficits combined. Holdings also advanced by 15.1 Moz in June alone to establish the series high, following a comparatively stable March-to-May interval. These comparisons do not prove that the accounting deficits are misstated: a market deficit is a flow balance and can be financed through inventory mobilization, whereas the London series records metal held in one visible location. They do show, however, that persistent deficits have coexisted with a large increase in reported London stocks rather than an observable drawdown there. With forecast mine output broadly flat at 844.1 Moz in 2026, down 0.3%, the London inflow is more plausibly evidence of inventory relocation, mobilization from previously unreported stocks, investment-product flows, imports, or changes in custody and ownership than of a contemporaneous surge in mine supply.

The monthly sequence also provides a clear quantitative footprint for the World Silver Survey 2026 statement that the October 2025 liquidity squeeze "subsequently eased as metal returned to London." LBMA holdings rose by 53.8 Moz in October, from 790.309 Moz to 844.105 Moz. A further 30.0 Moz arrived in November and 20.3 Moz in December, taking year-end holdings to 894.357 Moz. The cumulative November–December increase of 50.3 Moz nearly matched the October jump. This pattern is strongly consistent with the Survey's account: a sharp replenishment during the squeeze period was followed by continued rebuilding as dislocated metal moved into the London system. It should be treated as corroboration rather than a complete causal demonstration, because monthly vault totals do not identify the metal's origin, beneficial owner, encumbrance, or availability for lending. Even so, the timing and magnitude make the "metal returned to London" explanation more persuasive than an interpretation in which the squeeze eased despite continuing depletion of London's visible pool.

Against the published framework, the result is therefore a partial break, not a full falsification. The memo stated that the inventory-and-liquidity interpretation would be confirmed if London available stocks and COMEX deliverable inventories declined persistently, lease rates remained elevated, and regional physical premiums recurred or persisted. The observed London direction is the opposite: reported holdings rebuilt and reached a high. This weakens the inventory-depletion limb and suggests that the 2025 squeeze may have reflected location, timing, ownership, or lendability constraints rather than an absolute shortage of silver. However, "reported holdings" and "available stocks" are not interchangeable. Metal can be allocated, ETF-owned, operationally immobilized, or otherwise unavailable to the lease market. A larger gross stock can therefore coexist with tight marginal liquidity if only a limited portion is mobilizable on acceptable terms. The single SLV reading of 486.467 Moz as of 5 August 2026 underscores the potential importance of ownership structure, but without a comparable time series it cannot establish whether ETF flows supplied or absorbed the London rebuild.

The next evidence must distinguish inventory abundance from liquidity abundance. A new reading centered on easing physical availability would be confirmed if London holdings remain elevated or continue to rise while silver lease rates normalize across tenors, COMEX registered or otherwise deliverable stocks rebuild, regional bar and coin premiums subside, and delivery conditions remain orderly. A flow decomposition showing metal arriving from non-London inventories, imports, recycling, or investment liquidation would further support relocation and mobilization as the mechanism financing the deficit. Conversely, the apparent easing would be broken if the headline vault total remains high but lease rates re-escalate, COMEX deliverable inventories fall persistently, regional premiums recur, or evidence shows that most incremental London metal is allocated or otherwise unavailable for lending and settlement. Renewed London drawdowns after June would also indicate that the rebuild was temporary rather than structural.

The principal source is the official LBMA monthly loco-London vault-holdings series, pulled on 7 August 2026, with observations through June 2026. Deficit and mine-supply figures, and the description of the October 2025 squeeze, are from the World Silver Survey 2026 as cited in the published challenge memo. The analysis is limited by the absence of newly retrieved lease-rate curves, contemporaneous COMEX deliverable-stock data, regional premium series, and a full ETF-flow history. LBMA totals measure metal in vaults, not free float or lending availability, and monthly observations cannot resolve intramonth stress. On current evidence, the series high clearly weakens the proposition that persistent deficits are depleting visible London inventories, while leaving the broader liquidity question open pending lease-market, COMEX, premium, and ownership evidence.
